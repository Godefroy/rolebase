To create an org chart, list the roles in your company and who each one reports to, pick the format that answers your team's most common question, draw the boxes in a template or a tool, check the result with the people who hold the roles, then share it and update it at every change. An [org chart](/en/glossary/organigramme), or organizational chart, is a diagram of a company's structure: its roles, its teams and the reporting lines between them. This article details the five steps and gives you two free org chart templates to fill in, one for Word and one for PowerPoint.

## Why your company needs an org chart

An org chart shows each person where they fit, who they report to and who to turn to with a question. New hires learn the structure by reading it rather than by asking around. Managers spot two people doing the same job, or a responsibility nobody holds, and can fix it before it slows a project down. Employees also see the paths open to them for a promotion.

In a small team, everyone knows who does what. Once teams and managers multiply, the chart is where people check who is in charge of what, which is why it matters most in a growing company.

## What an org chart shows

An org chart shows two kinds of information: the roles and their responsibilities, and the reporting lines between them.

### Roles and responsibilities

Each box names a position and what it is in charge of. At a furniture manufacturer, the chart might show that the workshop manager oversees production while the purchasing manager sources the materials. Each employee then knows what they are responsible for, so two people rarely end up doing the same task.

### Reporting lines

The lines between the boxes show the management levels and who reports to whom. In a marketing agency, the CEO sits at the top, with the heads of marketing, sales and communications on the row below, then their team leaders and staff. Everyone can see their direct manager and who to contact when that manager is away.

## How to create an org chart, step by step

### 1. List the roles and who each one reports to

Start from the roles rather than the names, since one person can hold two roles. For each role, write down which role it reports to, who holds it and what it decides alone. Collect this information team by team, part-time staff included, through a short questionnaire or a conversation with each manager. Both of our free templates start with this list to fill in.

### 2. Choose the format

Choose the [type of org chart](/en/blog/org-chart-types) that answers the question your teams ask most often:

- A hierarchy chart, or hierarchical tree, shows who reports to whom.
- A functional org chart shows which department does which work.
- A matrix org chart shows who works for two managers.
- A circle org chart shows which team sits inside which.

The layout also depends on the kind of organization. In a nonprofit, the board and officers sit above the [staff, committees and volunteers who run its programs](/en/blog/nonprofit-org-chart). To see how an organization like yours lays out its chart, browse these [org chart examples for a startup, an SME, a nonprofit or a cooperative](/en/blog/org-chart-examples).

### 3. Draw the chart

Put the top role at the top, its direct reports on the row below, and keep one row per level so the reporting lines read from top to bottom. Most org charts use this layout, called a [hierarchical tree](/en/blog/hierarchy-chart). Give each level or each department its own shape or color, keep the titles short, and split a chart that grows too wide into one page per team.

A template saves you the layout work. With a dedicated tool, the chart also updates when a role changes.

### 4. Check it with the people who hold the roles

A chart you draw alone at your desk usually needs corrections the first time your team reads it. Go through each team's boxes with the people in them, then ask a colleague from another team to find who handles a given topic. If they hesitate, rename the boxes until the answer is obvious.

### 5. Share it and keep it up to date

Publish the chart where your team already looks for information, such as the intranet or the shared drive, and name the person in charge of updating it. Update it at each promotion, departure or hire, and send a short message to the people it affects. When responsibilities move from one role to another, tell the people concerned what they now handle and give them time to take it over.

## Tools to create an org chart

You can use two kinds of tools: the office software you already have, or a tool built to keep an org chart up to date.

### Office tools

You can draw an org chart with SmartArt in Word, PowerPoint or Excel. Pick Word for a chart you print or attach to a handbook, PowerPoint for a chart you present, and Excel to start from the table of roles you already keep in a spreadsheet.

<Callout type="warning" title="An org chart file goes out of date at the first change in the team">
A Word, PowerPoint or Excel file shows the company on the day it was saved. Each hire, departure or new role means reopening the file, moving the boxes and sending the new version around. That job usually falls to one person. After a few months, several versions circulate and nobody knows which one is right. A [dynamic org chart](/en/blog/dynamic-org-chart) updates as soon as you edit a role, so the whole team reads the same version.
</Callout>

Put the top role in a box at the top, its direct reports on the row below, then each manager's team on the next row, and connect every box to the one it reports to. A good org chart has one box per role, with a title that says what the role does and the name of the person who holds it. Its reporting lines read from top to bottom, each team fits on one page, and the chart shows the date of its last update. Anyone in the company should find who to ask without help.
