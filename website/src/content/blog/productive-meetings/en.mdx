Meetings were the bane of my first twelve years in corporate life. Then I spent an hour inside a self-managed team, watched fifteen items go by, each one leaving with an action and a name attached, and walked out trying to work out what had changed.

The difference was the mandate. Every item carried a precise question, and someone in the room had the authority to answer it. The agenda, the facilitation and the timing all served that mandate instead of standing in for it.

That is the argument of this article. Most useless meetings suffer from a missing mandate rather than from weak facilitation. Nobody knows what the session is supposed to produce, nobody has the authority to settle anything, and the slot comes back every week because deleting it would take a decision nobody feels entitled to make. A good facilitator makes those meetings pleasant. They stay useless.

This article is about the ordinary team meeting, the one you drop into a calendar with no framework attached. The codified formats have their own written process and their own article: the [tactical meeting](/en/blog/tactical-meeting) for moving current work forward, the [governance meeting](/en/blog/governance-meeting) for changing roles and rules.

## What a recurring slot actually costs

Steven Rogelberg, organizational psychology professor at the University of North Carolina at Charlotte, cites an estimate of 55 million meetings held every day in the United States in _The Surprising Science of Meetings_ (Oxford University Press, 2019).

Leslie Perlow, Constance Hadley and Eunice Eun measured what that takes at the top of an organization in the [July 2017 Harvard Business Review](https://hbr.org/2017/07/stop-the-meeting-madness). Executives spend close to 23 hours a week in meetings, up from under 10 hours in the 1960s. Their survey of 182 managers found 71% who call their meetings unproductive, 65% who say meetings keep them from doing their own work, and 64% who say meetings come at the expense of thinking time.

Michael Mankins, of Bain & Company, put a number on a single meeting in the May 2014 Harvard Business Review. Analysing how one large company spent its time, he found that a weekly executive committee meeting consumed 300,000 working hours a year across the organization, of which 7,000 were executive hours. Everything else went into preparation, analysis and a cascade of pre-meetings.

Your team plays at a different scale, and the order of magnitude still lands. A weekly one-hour slot with eight people burns close to 400 working hours a year before anyone prepares anything. Plenty of organizations debate the price of train tickets and let that slot live untouched for six years.

## Three questions before you open a slot

### What question does the session answer?

Write the item as a question rather than a topic. "Marketing budget" tells nobody anything. "Which line do we cut to cover the 30,000 missing from the marketing budget?" tells each person whether to come, what to bring, and when the session is over. Rogelberg makes this case in the [February 2020 Harvard Business Review](https://hbr.org/2020/02/how-to-create-the-perfect-meeting-agenda), with a test that settles things fast: when no question comes to mind, the meeting has no reason to exist.

### Who has the authority to answer it?

A question with no addressee produces a round of opinions. Name the person or the role who decides, and check that they will be there. When the answer belongs to someone who is away, the meeting turns into preparation for a decision made elsewhere, which is a fair use of everyone's time as long as you say so up front.

### What breaks if nobody meets?

A good share of agenda items work perfectly well as a written message. Status updates qualify, so do announcements, so do shared numbers. Save the synchronous hour for subjects where people need to answer each other: a contested call, a new problem nobody knows how to frame yet, friction between two people.

## Write a goal people can read

Rogelberg draws a conclusion from his research that contradicts the most common advice in the genre: having an agenda does nothing on its own for meeting effectiveness. What changes the outcome is what the agenda says and what it commits people to.

A useful item holds three things, the question asked, the type of output expected, and the time you give it.

| What the item asks for | The question written in the agenda                    | What leaves the room                              |
| ---------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| A decision             | "Which vendor do we pick for the redesign?"            | A dated decision, with the name of who made it     |
| An unblock             | "What is missing to ship the Martin mockups?"          | A next action, with an owner and a due date        |
| An exploration         | "Why does every client onboarding slip by three days?" | Written leads and the person who digs into them    |
| An announcement        | None, this item leaves the agenda                      | A written message sent the day before              |

Share that agenda at least a day ahead, with an open section where anyone adds their items. A team that discovers the agenda on arrival improvises, and a team that gets it one hour before pretends to have read it.

Rough timings sit on the same line as the question. Count twenty minutes for a contested call, five for an unblock, ten for an exploration. Those numbers get revised in the room, and announcing them gives the facilitator the right to cut without ruling on the substance.

## Who comes, and who stays out

Marcia Blenko, Michael Mankins and Paul Rogers set out a simple rule in _Decide & Deliver_ (Harvard Business Review Press, 2010), based on the decision data Bain & Company collected. Past seven people in a group that decides, each extra participant cuts decision effectiveness by around 10%. At seventeen, the group decides very little.

The practical consequence is uncomfortable, and it goes down fine when you prepare it.

- Invite people for an item rather than for an hour. Someone concerned by the third subject shows up at 10:20 and leaves at 10:35.
- Tell the people you are dropping that the notes reach them the same day. Silent removal reads as being pushed aside, announced removal reads as respect for their time.
- Give everyone the right to decline by explaining what they have nothing to add on. A team where nobody ever declines an invitation has a safety problem rather than a calendar problem.
- Give the person who only has information to pass on a written channel, and keep the seat for someone who has an answer to give.

## Mandate beats facilitation

A meeting without a mandate gives itself away on the way out. "Let's pick this up next week." It comes back in three situations, and none of them is fixed by better facilitation.

The decision belongs to someone who was not in the room. The group spent two hours on a subject it has no authority to settle, and the next session will replay the same discussion while everyone waits for a ruling.

Nobody knows who owns the decision. Each person suspects it is someone else, everyone offers an opinion, and the output is a soft consensus that nobody defends once back at their desk.

The decision belongs to everyone. This is the expensive version, because it produces long, participatory, inconsequential meetings until somebody settles it unilaterally out of exhaustion.

The fix happens outside the slot. It means writing down who decides what, with what latitude, over what scope. [Defined roles](/en/blog/define-roles) with their accountabilities and domains do exactly that, and a team that has written them sees its meetings shrink without touching its facilitation. When the question is about the structure itself, about who holds which role and under which rules, it belongs to a [governance meeting](/en/blog/governance-meeting) rather than to Tuesday's team meeting.

## What holds an ordinary session together

With the mandate settled, a handful of habits do the rest of the work. Adopt them one at a time rather than all in the same month.

Keep the same blocks in the same order for a recurring meeting. A stable run sheet, say an opening round, the numbers, the agenda items, a closing round, lowers the mental load on participants, who know what comes next and help hold the pace without thinking about it.

Ask for the numbers to be updated before the session. Meeting time is for commenting on a gap, never for filling in a tracking sheet while eight people watch.

Appoint a facilitator and a secretary, and rotate both every few months. The facilitator hands out speaking time, cuts digressions short and watches the clock. The secretary makes sure each item leaves with an action and an owner, and writes down the decisions taken. Handing facilitation to someone other than the team lead changes the dynamic, because the person holding the process stops being the person holding the substance.

Open and close with a quick round. Each person says one sentence on arrival about where they are, and one sentence on the way out about what they take with them. "I am half here, the baby kept me up all night" saves six people from reading something into a silence. "I still don't see how to implement what we just decided, I'll call Sofia tomorrow" rescues a decision that was about to evaporate.

Finish at the announced time, including when one item is left. A prioritized agenda makes that discipline liveable, and teams that overrun every other week end up with participants who arrive late to compensate.

Ask participants what they think, because your own read is optimistic. A phone survey of more than 1,300 managers, cited by Rogelberg in the [January 2019 Harvard Business Review](https://hbr.org/2019/01/why-your-meetings-stink-and-what-to-do-about-it), found 79% who called the meetings they convened productive, against 56% for meetings convened by others. Whoever talks the most always leaves the room with the best impression.

## What the session leaves behind

Every item handled leaves with an action, a named owner and a date. An action with no name belongs to nobody, and an action with no date belongs to next week, forever.

The [meeting notes](/en/blog/meeting-minutes) gather the decisions taken, the actions assigned and the subjects left open. They go out the same day to participants and to the people who were away. Their main value shows up six months later, when someone goes looking for why the team ruled that way.

Follow-up happens in the tool where the work already lives, rather than in a separate document nobody reopens. The next session starts with the previous session's actions, which takes two minutes and makes the real completion rate visible.

## The meetings you delete

Ben Laker, Vijay Pereira, Ashish Malik and Pawan Budhwar tracked 76 companies with more than 1,000 employees that introduced meeting-free days, and published their results in the [January 2022 MIT Sloan Management Review](https://sloanreview.mit.edu/article/the-surprising-impact-of-meeting-free-days/). Employees reported productivity up 71% and cooperation up 55%, with better autonomy and satisfaction and lower stress. The authors see the best balance around two to three protected days a week.

Those figures rest on self-reported perceptions and their size calls for caution. The mechanism needs no study. A continuous morning lets someone finish a piece of work, while four free twenty-minute gaps between three meetings mostly let them reread their notes.

Three moves free that time up. Review every recurring team meeting once a quarter and make each one justify its existence. Set default durations of 25 or 50 minutes rather than 30 or 60, which leaves room to breathe between slots. Replace the weekly status meeting with a written update, keeping a monthly slot for the subjects that genuinely need a conversation. The wider subject of [meeting overload](/en/blog/meeting-overload) deserves its own treatment.

In Rolebase, a meeting belongs to a role and is made of ordered steps. Five step types exist: speaking round, threads, checklist, indicators and tasks. A template saves a reusable sequence, which gives a recurring meeting the same run sheet week after week without rebuilding it.

- An item that calls for a collective ruling becomes a proposal in the matching thread, with a vote by consent, unanimity or majority. Recorded [decisions](/en/docs/decisions) form the log a newcomer goes back to in order to understand how things currently work.
- When the meeting ends, the notes gather the date, the participants with their attendance, the notes from each step, the tasks created and the decisions taken. One button generates a one-paragraph summary, which you review before saving it.

Rolebase leaves the timing to the people in the room and puts no timer on the steps. The only marker is the end-of-meeting button, which starts animating once the planned time has passed.

## Frequently asked questions

<FaqList>

<FaqItem question="How do you make a team meeting productive?">

Start by checking that the meeting needs to exist. Write each item as a question the session answers, name the person who has the authority to answer it, and invite only the people who ask or answer one. During the session, a facilitator holds the time and a secretary makes sure every item leaves with an action, an owner and a date. Steven Rogelberg points out that an agenda is not enough by itself, since what changes the outcome is how it is written.

</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="How many people should attend a meeting?">

Seven at most when the item calls for a decision. Marcia Blenko, Michael Mankins and Paul Rogers, in _Decide & Deliver_ (Harvard Business Review Press, 2010), found that past seven participants each extra person cuts decision effectiveness by around 10%. Everyone else gets the notes the same day, or joins for the single item that concerns them.

</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="How do you write a meeting objective?">

Phrase it as a question with a checkable answer. "Which line do we cut to cover the 30,000 we are missing?" works, "budget update" says nothing. Add the type of output you expect, meaning a decision, an action or a list of leads, along with the time allowed. When no question comes to mind, handle the subject in writing.

</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="What do you do about attendees who have no business being there?">

Tell them before you take them off the invitation, and commit to sending the notes the same day. Offer a partial invitation to anyone concerned by a single item. Give everyone the right to decline by explaining what they have nothing to add on, and treat the first refusal as a good sign.

</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="Does a team meeting need a facilitator and a secretary?">

Both roles make a visible difference by the second session. The facilitator hands out speaking time, cuts digressions and holds the clock. The secretary guarantees that each item leaves with an assigned action, and records the decisions. Rotating them every few months keeps them separate from the team lead. In Rolebase, both responsibilities are written as roles in their own right.

</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="How is this different from a tactical or governance meeting?">

The ordinary team meeting has no imposed process, and you choose its structure. The [tactical meeting](/en/blog/tactical-meeting) follows the seven steps of article 3.2 of the Holacracy Constitution to move current work forward. The [governance meeting](/en/blog/governance-meeting) changes the structure itself, the roles, the accountabilities and the rules, through a written decision process. The principles in this article apply to all three, and the two codified formats add constraints of their own.

</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="How do you know a recurring meeting should be deleted?">

Look at what comes out of it. A meeting that produces no decision and no new action across three consecutive sessions is passing information along, which a written message does better. Review every recurring meeting once a quarter and ask each one which question it handles that nothing else handles.

</FaqItem>

## What to take away

The work that makes a meeting productive happens before you open it. A written question instead of a topic, one person with the authority to answer it, a short invite list, and a commitment that every item leaves with an action carrying a name.

The rest, the opening round, the rotating facilitator, finishing on time, makes the session liveable without inventing its usefulness. A team that gets the upstream part right first sees its meetings shrink, then sees a few of them disappear. That is the good sign.
