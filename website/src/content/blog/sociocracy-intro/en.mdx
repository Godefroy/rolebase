In 1968, Gerard Endenburg took over the electrical engineering firm his father had built in Rotterdam. Two years later he cut the headcount from 160 to 100 and turned the company into a laboratory. He wanted to find out whether a decision-making method he had learned in primary school could hold up in an industrial business. The experiment ran for ten years, and it produced sociocracy as organisations practise it today.

The word itself is a century older, and it has meant three different things along the way. That drift explains most of the confusion you find in introductions to the subject. Here is the actual lineage, the real mechanics behind the four principles, and what the model asks for in return.

## Where sociocracy comes from

Auguste Comte coined the term in 1851, in *Système de politique positive*. For Comte, sociocracy named the government of society by itself, inside his Religion of Humanity. The word blends the Latin *socius*, meaning companion, with the Greek *kratos*, meaning power. It translates as "rule by associates", rather than the "governance by equality" that circulates in many summaries.

American sociologist Lester Frank Ward picked the term up in an 1881 paper for the *Penn Monthly* and expanded it in *Dynamic Sociology* (1883). Ward described a society steered by scientific knowledge rather than by party competition. At that point sociocracy still belonged to political philosophy, with no application to running an organisation.

The move into practice came from a Dutch Quaker educator. In 1926, Kees Boeke and Betty Cadbury founded the Werkplaats Kindergemeenschap in Bilthoven, a school where pupils and teachers decided together how the place would run. Boeke called the pupils workers and the teachers co-workers, and decisions came out of regular talkover meetings where everyone had to agree. By 1945 the community counted around 400 pupils and adults, and Boeke published *Sociocracy: Democracy as It Might Be*, arguing that the same method could govern a whole society.

Gerard Endenburg attended that school during the war. An electrical engineer who had worked at Philips and picked up cybernetics during military service, he took over the family firm in 1968 and installed what he had lived through under Boeke. One problem showed up fast. Full consensus stalls the moment a single person digs in. Endenburg replaced it with consent, where a decision goes through for as long as no argued objection remains open. That single swap is what made the method workable in a company that has to ship. He built it out into the *Sociocratische Kringorganisatie Methode*, the sociocratic circle-organisation method, usually shortened to SKM or SCM, and founded the Sociocratisch Centrum around the turn of the 1980s. In 1992 he defended a doctoral thesis at the University of Twente, *Sociocratie als sociaal ontwerp*.

<Callout type="info" title="The Dutch works council exemption">

Organisations applying SKM have claimed an exemption from the Dutch works council requirement since 1994. The mechanism is narrower than the headline suggests. The Dutch Works Councils Act lets the Social and Economic Council grant such an exemption case by case, for renewable five-year periods, to a company that already informs and consults its employees through another channel. Sociocratic companies obtain it on application, one file at a time.

</Callout>

Four decades after Endenburg, two practitioners went back to the same foundations. James Priest and Bernhard Bockelbrink launched Sociocracy 3.0 in March 2015, with Liliana David joining shortly after. They kept consent and circles, folded in ideas from agile and lean, and above all cut the whole thing into independent patterns published under a Creative Commons licence. A team can take three of them and leave the rest.

## The four principles, in detail

### 1. Consent

A proposal passes once no argued objection remains open. An objection has to name a concrete impediment, a reason the proposal would stop the circle from delivering on its [purpose](/en/glossary/raison-detre). "I would prefer something else" is a preference, and a preference gets heard without blocking. The facilitator goes round the circle, collects objections, and the proposal is reworked until each one is resolved.

The difference with consensus fits in one line. Consensus looks for everyone's agreement, consent looks for the absence of an impediment. Decisions land faster, and they land on proposals that are "good enough for now, safe enough to try", to borrow the phrase Sociocracy 3.0 made popular. Our article on [consent decision-making](/en/blog/consent-decision) walks through the full sequence.

### 2. The circle

A circle is a team that owns a domain and decides inside it. It has a written purpose, [accountabilities](/en/glossary/redevabilite), members, and its own policy meetings where it sets its rules. Circles nest, with a general circle holding department circles that hold team circles.

The hierarchy of domains therefore stays. What goes away is the hierarchy of orders. A parent circle keeps responsibility for its domain and gets the child circle's agreement on the rules that apply inside it, thanks to the next principle.

### 3. Double linking

Two people connect a circle to its parent circle, and both of them sit in both circles, with consent rights in both.

The operational leader is appointed by the parent circle and carries its voice into the child circle. The delegate is elected by the child circle and carries its voice into the parent circle. Plenty of introductions describe the first link and stop there. The second one is what changes the power balance, because it travels upward and it consents on the same footing as every other member of the circle above.

The effect is mechanical. Since two members of the child circle hold consent rights in the parent circle, the parent circle adopts its policy decisions with their agreement. A single downward link would produce a transmission belt. Two links produce a feedback loop, which is precisely the cybernetic vocabulary Endenburg started from.

### 4. Election without candidates

A circle's elected roles, typically facilitator, secretary and delegate, are filled without anyone putting themselves forward. The sequence has five steps.

1. The circle rereads the role description, the term length and the qualities it is looking for, all of them agreed by consent beforehand.
2. Everyone writes one name on a slip, their own or somebody else's.
3. A round asks each person to explain their choice against the qualities listed.
4. A second round lets people change their nomination after hearing the arguments.
5. The facilitator proposes one person, and the circle consents or objects with reasons tied to skills and experience.

The process anchors the choice in fit for the role rather than appetite for it, and it makes the reasoning public, since everyone hears why a given person was picked. The trade-off is exposure. Sitting in a room and hearing why nobody nominated you takes a level of relational safety that most teams build slowly. Our article on [elections without candidates](/en/blog/election-without-candidate) covers the facilitation scripts.

## Classic sociocracy, Sociocracy 3.0, Holacracy

Three models descend from Endenburg's work, and conversations mix them up constantly.

Classic sociocracy, SKM, stays closest to the original. The Sociocracy Group in the Netherlands and Sociocracy For All in the United States both teach it, with no single owner, and double linking remains its backbone.

Sociocracy 3.0 keeps the principles and drops the "method" format. Each practice becomes a documented pattern adopted on its own, according to what the organisation is trying to improve. A team can run on consent and rounds for a year and leave its structure untouched. The resources are free to use, including commercially.

Holacracy went the other way. Brian Robertson formalised it in 2007 out of the practices at his company Ternary Software, then codified it in a constitution published in 2010. The frame tightens around one constitutional text, tightly scripted governance meetings and a registered trademark. Double linking becomes a lead link appointed by the parent circle and a rep link elected by the circle. The vocabulary changes, the upward mechanism stays close. Our [comparison of Holacracy and sociocracy](/en/blog/holacracy-vs-sociocracy) goes through the differences point by point.

## Where the model runs out of road

Sociocracy makes an argument about power, and that argument has blind spots its own practitioners document.

The facilitator holds more influence than the role suggests. They reframe proposals, they judge when an objection has been resolved, they pick the moment for the consent round. Jem Bendell, a British academic and founder of the Deep Adaptation Forum, calls this the framing effect, steering a decision while keeping the appearance of neutrality. Rotating the role and writing proposals before the meeting reduces the exposure.

The process eats time, and that time is unevenly available. Rounds, policy meetings and elections all take hours in the room. People with caring responsibilities or tight finances supply fewer of those hours, and carry less weight in decisions as a result. Bendell reads this as existing privilege reproducing itself in procedural clothing.

Silence can pass for agreement. A decision taken "with no objections" sometimes means nobody felt able to speak. Sociocracy For All states it plainly: the absence of an objection gets mistaken for consent, and the mistake turns harmful in a group where a few voices dominate. The model assumes everyone feels entitled to block the group, and that entitlement builds slowly in a team coming out of ten years of top-down management.

Procedure can become the subject. Bendell describes groups spending more energy checking that they are applying the method correctly than on the problem in front of them. The symptom shows up early, when meetings start being about meetings.

Then there is scope. Consent applies to policy decisions, the ones that set lasting rules for a domain. Operational decisions belong to whoever holds the role and get made alone, on the spot. Organisations that skip that distinction end up running a consent round on the colour of the pens, and conclude that sociocracy is slow.

## What to take away

Sociocracy took the best part of a century to travel from a philosopher's utopia to a working business method, and it got there by an unlikely route, a Dutch primary school followed by a factory used as a test bench for ten years. What holds up sits in two mechanisms. Consent gets decisions through that consensus buries. Double linking gives the lower circle a real say over the rules that bind it.

What it asks for gets said less often. It takes meeting time, a team willing to object, a facilitator watching their own influence, and a clean line between what the circle decides and what the role holder decides. Organisations that put those four conditions in place before the vocabulary last considerably longer.
