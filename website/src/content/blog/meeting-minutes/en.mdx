Minutes of meeting (MoM), or meeting minutes, are the written record of a meeting: who attended, what was decided and why, and who does what by when. Most minutes, written from memory the evening after the meeting, never get read. Use a meeting minutes template you fill in while people are still talking, so the minutes are finished when the meeting ends.

You get the template as a Word file and as plain text, plus an agenda template, a filled example, the format that keeps minutes findable a year later, and the method for filling the template in.

Good meeting minutes record the meeting date and attendees, follow the agenda, summarize the discussion that matters, state each decision and its reason, and list actions with a named owner and due date. Add the next meeting date when it is known. For a formal body, check its applicable rules before using a team-meeting template.

The file opens in Word, Google Docs, LibreOffice or Pages. To write your minutes straight into a wiki or a meeting tool, use the same structure in plain text.

```
Meeting:
Date:
Time:
Location or link:
Attendees:
Apologies:
Minutes taken by:

AGENDA
1.
2.
3.

DISCUSSION AND DECISIONS

Item 1
  Discussed:
  Decided:
  Why:

Item 2
  Discussed:
  Decided:
  Why:

ACTIONS
- <Action> | <Owner> | <Due date>
-
-

NEXT MEETING
Date:
Carried over:
```

**Why** stops a decision from being relitigated three months later by someone who was not in the room. **Owner** is one named person, never a team, because when everyone owns an action, each person assumes someone else will do it. If you run role-based meetings, the owner is a role rather than a job title, which keeps the action valid after the person changes. To find those decisions again from one meeting to the next, copy them into a [decision log](/en/blog/decision-log) as well.

### A Filled Example

This is the structure filled in after a 45-minute product meeting.

- **Meeting:** Product weekly
- **Date:** 11 March 2026
- **Time:** 10:00 to 10:45
- **Location or link:** video call
- **Attendees:** Ana, Ben, Chloe, Dan
- **Apologies:** Emma
- **Minutes taken by:** Ben

#### Agenda

1. Onboarding drop-off
2. Pricing page test
3. Release date

#### Discussion and decisions

##### Onboarding drop-off

- **Discussed:** 38% of new accounts stop at the invite step.
- **Decided:** make the invite step skippable.
- **Why:** the step blocks solo users who sign up to try the product alone.

##### Release date

- **Discussed:** the API migration is two days behind.
- **Decided:** keep 27 March, cut the CSV export from the release.
- **Why:** the date was announced to three customers, the export can wait for the next release.

#### Actions

| Action | Owner | Due date |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tell the three customers what the 27 March release contains | Chloe | 16 March |
| Make the invite step skippable | Ana | 18 March |

#### Next meeting

- **Date:** 18 March 2026
- **Carried over:** pricing page test

The example records decisions rather than the debate. Someone reading it in six months wants the decision, the reason and the owner, so that is all it holds.

## Why Teams Keep Minutes of Meeting

Once the meeting is over, the minutes are the only trace of what it decided. Teams go back to them in four situations:

- A newcomer, or someone covering for a colleague on leave, needs the history of a project without rebuilding it from chat threads.
- When someone reopens a decision months later, the minutes show what was decided and why.
- An audit or a process review asks for evidence of what the organization decided and when.
- People who missed the meeting catch up without interrupting a colleague in the middle of focused work.

Hybrid and remote teams depend most on minutes to let absent colleagues catch up on their own. When the answer is in shared minutes, they can act on it the same day instead of waiting for a reply. Other departments read the same minutes to see what a decision changes for their own work.

## How to Write Effective Meeting Minutes

The template gives you the structure. To fill it in well, prepare it before the meeting, take notes during it and follow up after. Each step is easier when you [run a meeting worth recording](/en/blog/productive-meetings).

### Before the Meeting

- Copy the agenda into the template, one block per item.
- Fill in the header with the date, the attendees, the apologies and the name of the person taking the minutes.

### During the Meeting

- Write down each decision, its reason and the actions as they come.
- Summarize only the part of the debate that led to each decision.
- Note who made a proposal or raised an objection when it shaped the decision.
- Ask for a decision to be restated when it is unclear, then ask who owns each action and by when.

### After the Meeting

- Reread the minutes the same day, check names and dates, and link the documents shown during the meeting.
- Send them to the attendees and to the people who could not come.
- Open the next meeting with the actions of the previous minutes, so each one is marked done, moved or dropped.

## How to Store Meeting Minutes So You Can Find Them

Minutes stay useful as long as you can find them, even a year later. Five habits make them easy to find:

- Use the same template for every meeting, so a series of minutes reads the same way.
- Name files on one pattern, for example `Minutes_Sales_2026-03-11`, so they sort by date.
- Keep them in one shared place (Google Drive, SharePoint, Notion or a meeting tool), filed by team or meeting type.
- Decide who can read them and who can edit them.
- File the minutes right after the meeting, while the details are fresh.
