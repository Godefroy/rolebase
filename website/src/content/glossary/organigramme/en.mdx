An org chart, short for organizational chart, is a diagram of how an organization is structured: the positions or roles it is made of, the people who fill them and the reporting lines between them. It answers two questions: who holds which position or role, and who reports to whom. You will also see it called an organization chart or an organogram.

## The most common types of org charts

- A hierarchical org chart, also called an [organizational tree](/en/blog/hierarchy-chart), places each position under the one it reports to, from leadership at the top down to the teams. It is the most common type.
- A functional org chart groups positions by function, such as finance, sales or production.
- A [matrix org chart](/en/blog/matrix-organizational-structure) adds a second reporting line, usually a project or a market, next to the function.
- A circle org chart nests teams inside one another. Organizations that run [holacracy](/en/glossary/holacratie) or [sociocracy](/en/glossary/sociocratie) often use it.
- A [photo directory](/en/blog/photo-directory) shows each person with their photo, name and position.

Each [type of org chart](/en/blog/org-chart-types) answers a different question: who reports to whom, which department does which work, who works for two managers, which team sits inside which, who is who. Pick the one that answers the question your teams ask most often.

## What an org chart shows and what it leaves out

Each box of an org chart usually holds a job title and the name of the person in that job, sometimes with a photo, a department or a location. The lines between the boxes show who reports to whom. A new hire reads it to find their manager, their team and the person to ask in another department.

A chart of positions shows little of the work each person actually does. It leaves out the people who wear several hats as well as the teams that work across departments. It also goes out of date quickly, because it is usually drawn in Word or PowerPoint and stays as it was after the next reorganization.

## Org charts built from roles

In a role-based organization, the org chart maps [roles](/en/glossary/role) rather than positions. Each role has a [purpose](/en/glossary/raison-detre), a [domain](/en/glossary/domaine) and [accountabilities](/en/glossary/redevabilite). One person can hold several roles across several teams. The chart then shows who does what, as well as who reports to whom.

## Frequently asked questions

<FaqList>

<FaqItem question="Is an org chart mandatory?">

In France, no law requires a company to have one. The official list of the information an employer must display or give to employees, on [service-public.gouv.fr](https://entreprendre.service-public.gouv.fr/vosdroits/F23106), covers items such as safety instructions, working hours and collective agreements, with no mention of an org chart. An org chart still helps new hires find their manager and the person to ask in another department.

</FaqItem>

<FaqItem question="Is a company org chart confidential?">

The company decides. Many companies share their org chart with every employee and publish their leadership team on their website. In France, an org chart counts as a trade secret only if it meets the three conditions of [article L151-1 of the Commercial Code](https://www.legifrance.gouv.fr/codes/article_lc/LEGIARTI000037266553): it is hard to find, it has commercial value because it is secret, and the company takes reasonable steps to protect it. A chart that names employees also holds personal data, which the GDPR protects.

</FaqItem>

</FaqList>
