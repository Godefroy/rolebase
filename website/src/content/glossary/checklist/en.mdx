A "checklist" is an organized and structured tool in the form of a list, used as an operational guide to ensure that all necessary tasks, steps, or items are completed systematically and thoroughly. Checklists are widely used across various fields to ensure consistency, accuracy, and compliance in the execution of processes, procedures, or projects.

Checklists can vary in size and complexity, ranging from simple [task](/en/glossary/tache) lists to detailed documents covering multiple aspects of an operation. They are commonly used in healthcare, aviation, project management, maintenance, safety, quality assurance, and many other sectors. Checklists help track and document progress, reduce human errors, facilitate training and onboarding, and ensure compliance with standards and protocols.

In summary, a "checklist" is a structured list that serves as an operational guide to ensure that all necessary tasks or steps are completed systematically. It is an essential tool for ensuring quality, compliance, and efficiency in processes and operations across many professional fields.

## What makes a checklist work

A checklist earns its place when it stays short enough to be read every time it is used. Each step is written as an action, follows the order the work happens in, and is verifiable, so anyone can tell whether it is done. A checklist that grows past a page tends to be skipped, which is why the ones used in aviation and surgery cover only the steps that matter when something goes wrong.

The other condition is ownership. A checklist maintained by whoever performs the work stays accurate, because the person who spots a missing step is the person who can add it. When nobody owns it, it drifts from reality and people quietly stop following it.

## Checklists in an organization

Inside a team, a checklist usually belongs to a role rather than to a person. Onboarding a new colleague, closing a month, publishing a release, running a recurring meeting: each of these is a repeatable sequence attached to a responsibility. Writing it down as a checklist makes the work transferable, so a handover means passing on the list instead of transferring years of habit.

This is where checklists connect to [defining clear roles](/en/blog/define-roles). A role with explicit accountabilities can hold its own checklist, and the two stay in sync as the organization changes. In [Rolebase](/en/features), a checklist is a field on the role itself, and a recurring meeting can open a checklist step that pulls it up for whoever holds that role, so the list is reviewed as part of the work rather than kept in a personal notes app.
