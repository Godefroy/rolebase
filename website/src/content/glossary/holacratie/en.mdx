Holacracy is a method of self-management in which authority belongs to defined roles rather than to managers, under rules written in a constitution the whole organization adopts. The constitution also sets how roles are created and changed. The American entrepreneur [Brian Robertson](/en/glossary/brian-robertson) developed it in the 2000s at Ternary Software, his software company in Pennsylvania, drawing on sociocracy to fix the problems he ran into as head of that company.

## How holacracy works

Work is split into [roles](/en/glossary/role), each with a [purpose](/en/glossary/raison-detre), [accountabilities](/en/glossary/redevabilite) and sometimes a [domain](/en/glossary/domaine). One person usually holds several roles. Roles are grouped into [circles](/en/glossary/cercle), which can themselves contain other circles, up to the one covering the whole organization.

Each circle holds two kinds of meetings. Members change the circle's roles and policies in [governance meetings](/en/glossary/reunion-de-gouvernance) and handle day-to-day work in [tactical meetings](/en/glossary/reunion-tactique). In both, anyone can raise a [tension](/en/glossary/tension), a gap between how things work today and how they could work. A governance proposal passes unless someone raises a valid objection. This rule is close to [consent](/en/glossary/consentement) in [sociocracy](/en/glossary/sociocratie).

## Criticism of holacracy

Holacracy is heavy, which is the price of its precision. The constitution runs to many pages and the meeting formats are strict, so a team needs months to learn them. The online shoe retailer Zappos, its best-known adopter, rolled it out to the whole company in 2014. In 2015, 18% of its employees took the severance offered to those who preferred to leave. Many teams now borrow roles and tensions from holacracy without adopting the full constitution.
