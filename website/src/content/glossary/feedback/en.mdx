Feedback is information given to a person about their work, behaviour or results, based on what the giver observed, so that the person can adjust. It differs from an opinion or a judgement because it ties a specific fact to its effect: "the report arrived the morning of the meeting, so nobody had read it" rather than "you're always late".

Feedback can be spoken or written, informal or part of a formal review. Positive feedback tells someone what to keep doing. Corrective feedback asks for a change. Both work better when they come soon after the fact.

## What makes feedback useful

- It describes a dated fact rather than a personality trait.
- It says what that fact changed for the giver, the team or the client.
- It concerns something the person can act on.
- It opens a conversation, since the person receiving it often knows part of the story the giver missed.

Structured methods help people follow these points when the conversation is difficult. The [OSCAR method](/en/glossary/feedback-oscar) goes from the observation of facts to their consequence, then to the action and the expected results. SBI, developed by the Center for Creative Leadership, follows a similar path: situation, behaviour, impact.

## Where feedback shows up at work

Companies collect feedback in performance reviews, from clients in quality management, and from users in product development. In teams that distribute authority through [roles](/en/glossary/role), feedback between peers takes over part of what the annual review with a manager used to do.
